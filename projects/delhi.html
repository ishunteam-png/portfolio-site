SATALITE Delhi

Strict EGMS-L3 road-subsidence pilot — Najafgarh Road, SW Delhi

A strict EGMS-L3 equivalent road-subsidence map — vertical and east-west deformation rates per pixel, ITRF14 absolute frame, ERA5 tropospheric correction, two-track decomposition. End to end on a local Linux workstation with open-source tools. InSAR / remote-sensing engineer, freelance, April–May 2026.

WHAT I BUILT

The site choice itself was the first piece of work. I went into this expecting to pilot at Visakhapatnam — coastal subsidence is the obvious story for an Indian road. A 30-second ASF catalog check killed that: no ascending Sentinel-1 coverage at all. Without ASC + DSC you can’t do strict EGMS-L3 decomposition. Mumbai and Bangalore had the same gap. That left Delhi and Kolkata as the two dual-track-covered Indian metros I knew of. I picked Delhi — well-documented groundwater subsidence in southwest Delhi in the literature, inland (no coastal geoid effects to fight during atmospheric correction), and Najafgarh Road in Sector 8 Dwarka is real road infrastructure the client can point to.

Data: 165 Sentinel-1 SLCs across 3 years. ASC relative orbit 27, IW2, 88 acquisitions. DSC relative orbit 136, IW1, 77 acquisitions. ~12-day cadence (S1A only — S1B failed end-2021 and S1C didn’t come online for the Delhi pass until mid-2025, so the late-window frames are sparse and I treated the whole stack as 1A-cadence for the SBAS network design). The geometric point that mattered later: ASC sits west of the AOI looking east, DSC sits east looking west — antiparallel horizontal LOS vectors with similar incidence angles, ~33°. That’s what makes the 2×2 V_U+V_E decomposition well-conditioned.

Workstation and software. 12-core / 32 GB / 2 TB SSD on Ubuntu 22.04. SNAP for the per-pair InSAR work — bumped the JVM heap from the default 8 GB to 20 GB in ~/snap/etc/snap.properties because the default crashes on coregistration of large bursts. Python 3.10 conda env with MintPy + SNAPHU + asf_search + sentineleof + rasterio + geopandas + pyproj + h5py from conda-forge; PyAPS3 from GitHub (it’s not on conda-forge). I never touch h5py manually after the one time I mixed pip and conda installs and segfaulted MintPy at load_data.

SBAS network. Each acquisition pairs with the three nearest forward neighbours, max temporal baseline 60 days. Past 60 days Delhi’s monsoon decorrelation is too severe in my experience to be worth processing. 258 ASC + 218 DSC = 476 pairs.

SNAP per pair. TOPS coregistration with ESD, interferogram formation, topo phase removal against Copernicus 30 m DEM, deburst, 35×6 multilook (~80 m pixels), Goldstein filter, SNAPHU unwrap, terrain correction to UTM 43N. Built once in SNAP Graph Builder, saved as XML, run via gpt for every pair. I split the graph into two halves around the SNAPHU export/import — SNAP’s built-in SNAPHU wrapper stalled silently on three different pairs early in the run with no useful log entry, and running SNAPHU directly on the command line at least lets me see when it’s thrashing on tile boundaries.

ERA5 atmospheric correction. Tropospheric water vapour adds ~1–2 cm peak-to-peak LOS noise over Delhi across a year. Without correction it bleeds into the velocity as a fake annual cycle, especially for any pixel near vegetation. I batched CDS requests overnight — 01:00 UTC for descending (~06:00 local pre-dawn), 13:00 UTC for ascending (~18:00 local). About 300 GRIB files.

MintPy SBAS. Separate workspaces mintpy_asc/ and mintpy_dsc/. Mixing both into one workspace works in principle but debugging is painful once the geometry files start overlapping. modify_network dropped 5 ASC pairs + 1 DSC pair against minCoherence = 0.5, preserving the MST. reference_point landed on a high-coherence rooftop on the AOI’s eastern edge in both stacks (same physical structure — reassuring). correct_troposphere with the ERA5 step is the slow one, ~25 minutes per stack. Residual RMS settled at ~2 mm in both stacks; EGMS noise floor is ~2–3 mm so I called this good enough.

ITRF14 tie via the Indian-plate Euler pole. MintPy gives relative velocities (reference pixel = 0). EGMS L3 wants absolute ITRF14. With no co-located GNSS at the AOI I used Altamimi et al. (2017)’s plate-motion model to compute the expected ITRF14 velocity at my reference rooftop and subtracted it from every pixel. Pole at φ = 50.295°N, λ = −8.155°E, Ω = 0.554°/Myr; at my AOI that’s V_E ≈ +40 and V_N ≈ +39 mm/yr, V_U = 0 by rigid-plate assumption.

Validation against MIDAS-IGS14. Three GNSS stations — LCK4 (Lucknow, 427 km), HYDE (Hyderabad, 1251 km), IISC (Bangalore, 1732 km). Horizontal residuals coherent at the −4 to −6 mm/yr east and −4 mm/yr north level across a 1,700 km span. Almost certainly a small bias in the Altamimi 2017 Indian-plate pole (likely tightened up in ITRF2020 — I didn’t redo the calculation with the 2020 pole because by the time it occurred to me I was already through decomposition; one for a future run). The LCK4 vertical residual is well-documented Lucknow groundwater subsidence, not a frame issue.

Decomposition. Sentinel-1’s polar orbit gives the V_N coefficient under 5% of total, so dropping V_N is the standard move. Solved the remaining 2×2 (V_E, V_U) system per-pixel by Cramer’s rule; the per-pixel determinant rides at ~0.95 across the AOI (anything above 0.3 is usable, 0.95 is comfortable). Wrote the determinant out as its own raster for the QA dashboard tab.

PS mask. Average spatial coherence ≥ 0.70 in both stacks — the EGMS rule. Mean ASC 0.83 (range 0.53–0.97), mean DSC 0.88 (0.55–0.98). Pixels passing in both: 2,555 PS. I tested 0.75 and dropped to ~2,100 — tighter felt like wasted observations, the AOI was already cleanly populated at 0.70.

“Acceptable for this AOI; would not generalise.”
THE BITS THAT BIT ME

Multilook calibration. First batch was 30×5 looks. A few pairs in I noticed the result over the road corridor was smeared — small-target PS getting averaged into their neighbours. Reran a subset at 35×6 (~80×84 m), visibly cleaner, locked it in. EGMS L3 is at 100 m formally; 80 m gave me a slightly finer grid without exploding compute.

SNAP JVM OOM. The pattern was always the same: a large burst pair, four gpt processes running in parallel, OS killing the JVM. After the second time I capped parallel at three concurrent pairs and the crashes stopped. Throughput dropped from ~5 pairs/hour to ~4. I lived with it.

ESD threshold on a few pairs. Default ESD coherence threshold is 0.3. A handful of pairs failed there; I dropped to 0.2 for those specific pairs and they coregistered fine. Not ideal — a lower threshold means trusting a noisier azimuth-shift estimate — but the AOI itself was high-coherence even though the broader burst had a low-coherence stretch. Acceptable for this AOI; would not generalise.

ERA5 zero-byte GRIBs. Three GRIB files came back zero bytes from the CDS endpoint and tropo_pyaps3 segfaulted on one of them three days later, when MintPy tried to read it. Error was uninformative — “Segmentation fault” with no traceback. I now eccodes-check every GRIB after download and run a cfgrib.open_dataset() sanity probe before anything reaches MintPy.

Mid-stack shape mismatch. Pair 20240623_20240705, a monsoon-window pair, had a slightly different raster size after deburst — probably because SNAP’s auto burst-selection caught a different sub-burst due to a slight orbit shift. Regenerated that one pair separately with explicit firstBurstIndex / lastBurstIndex matching the rest of the stack and the rerun was clean. I didn’t dig further once it worked.

2024 SAFE-layout shift. ASF’s cloud-native rollout in 2024 changed the inner directory layout of the newer SLCs slightly. SNAP’s default Read node initially refused to recognise a handful of the newer scenes. A small file-pattern tweak in the SNAP graph fixed it — lost ~an hour figuring out it wasn’t a corrupt download.
